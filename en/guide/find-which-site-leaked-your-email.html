Which Website Leaked Your Email? Use One Address per Site to Spot the Source

One day an email arrives from a company you never subscribed to, and the first question is: who gave out my address? If one inbox has been typed into dozens of forms, it is nearly impossible to say. This guide has two steps: find clues in the mail you already have, then use one address per site so the source is obvious next time.

Why it is so hard to find the culprit

Once your main email is typed into a form, it can end up on marketing lists, be reused in partner promotions, or be exposed in a data breach. After years of signing up everywhere with one address, every service is a suspect and nothing tells them apart.

The root cause is that every site receives the same address. To trace anything later, each site has to get a different one. Before that, see what the mail in front of you can tell you.

02

Step 1: read the clues in the unwanted email

  1. Read the footer. Legitimate marketing mail often says “you are receiving this because you signed up at …”, the most direct clue to the source.
  2. Check the sender and link domains. The sender name and the domain behind the unsubscribe link usually point to the company, or its mailing platform, that actually sent it.
  3. Recall the timing. Roughly when did these emails start? What did you sign up for, download or attend around then?
  4. Do not click “unsubscribe” or load images. On mail from an unknown source, either action can confirm to the sender that your address is active. Reading the text is enough.
Clues are not proof

Mailing lists are often shared between companies, so the name on the email may not be the first party that exposed you. Treat it as a suspect, not a verdict.

03

Step 2: use one address per site from now on

The idea is simple: give every site you do not fully trust its own separate temporary address, and let the address itself record who got it. CleanTempMail’s web inbox is free, needs no sign-up, and supports random addresses or a custom prefix, so you can put the site name in the prefix.

  1. Open CleanTempMail. Choose a custom prefix, enter a short word that identifies the site, such as whitepaper-acme, and pick one of the available domains.
  2. Use that address in the site’s form. One site, one address; never share one across sites.
  3. Log the mapping. A table with address, site and date is your source lookup sheet.
  4. Come back to check. The site’s help centre explains that you can open an inbox directly through a copied link; bookmark it to look for new mail later.
  5. Drop it when something is off. If an address starts receiving promotions unrelated to that site, you know where to look, and you simply stop using it. Your other addresses are unaffected.

Avoid names and birthdays in the prefix. To make an address harder to guess, add a few random characters after the site name.

04

What this can and cannot do

  • It can: keep stray promotions in a temporary inbox instead of your main one, and tell you which site’s address a message was sent to.
  • It cannot: prove the site leaked anything. Addresses on a shared domain can be guessed or collected elsewhere, so a message is a clue, not evidence.
  • Keep in mind: the privacy notes on the official site say mail is temporary and not stored permanently. If you need a message as evidence, screenshot or save it while it is there.

05

Accounts that should not use a temporary address

Tracing only works if you are willing to go back and check that address, so it suits short-term cases: downloads, trying a tool, event sign-ups. For payments, orders, banking, or anything needing password recovery, use a mailbox you control long term. The trade-offs are in temporary vs permanent email.

Also note that some sites restrict temporary addresses; respect their rules.

06

Frequently asked questions

Can I be 100% sure which site leaked my email?

No. Clues in the email and separate addresses only narrow it down; lists get resold, and whether a leak occurred needs more evidence.

Why use a different address for each site?

Only with different addresses can you tell which site’s address an unexpected email was sent to, and sites cannot link you together by one shared address.

Custom prefix or random address?

For tracing, use a custom prefix that maps to the site. Keep names and birthdays out of it and add a few random characters at the end to make it harder to guess.

Will the email still be there later?

Not necessarily. The official site says mail is temporary and not stored permanently; save anything you need as evidence while you use the inbox.

Should I click unsubscribe on unknown mail?

Not on mail from an unknown source. For a temporary address, simply no longer using it is the easiest way out.
